David Adelani’s education passes through Abeokuta and Abuja before Saarland. His work now includes a research group at McGill and Mila in Montréal, focused on languages that larger technology systems have often served poorly. A map of his work affiliation places him in Canada. A map of his education connects him to Nigeria and Germany. The research itself reaches across those borders. [1] [2]

Chika Yinka-Banjo’s documented route leads from universities in Nigeria to postgraduate study in South Africa and a leadership role at the University of Lagos’s AI and Robotics Lab. There is no need to leave the continent to follow that trail. [3] [4]

These are useful starting points because they complicate the question. If a government wants to know where its researchers are employed, it needs work affiliations. If a university wants to understand its contribution, it needs education and subsequent careers. If a company wants to hire, it needs available skills and working arrangements. A single map cannot answer all three.

The economic question is where the conditions exist for people to turn knowledge into sustained work: laboratories that can support a research programme, employers that can commission and deploy systems, and institutions through which that work benefits others. Location matters, but the unit we choose to locate matters just as much.

Visibility is already a selection.

Deep Learning Indaba reports IndabaX activity across 47 countries in 2025. That is a much wider community footprint than a handful of familiar company names or university biographies can show. It describes programme reach; it gives us no headcount for the AI workforce in those countries. [5]

The atlas below begins with public, attributable records. It consequently favours people whose careers are easy to find: academics with institutional profiles, founders with company biographies and practitioners recognised by a programme. That selection is consequential. An engineer working inside a bank may be less visible than a researcher with a university page. Visibility should not quietly become our definition of talent.

    Inspect the people behind the view

    Follow the work

    A career can connect several economies.

    An overseas affiliation tells us where a role is anchored. It does not, on its own, tell us who uses the resulting research, whether collaborations continue, or whether that role will be permanent.

    Different forms of connection create different opportunities. A research partnership can move knowledge without moving an entire team. A local appointment can give students a supervisor and a research agenda. A company can use a distributed team to serve customers in several markets. Establishing which of those connections actually produces durable local value requires evidence about projects, employment and institutions over time.

    A broader labour-market signal

    Mobility needs a different dataset.

    A World Bank analysis of LinkedIn high-skilled digital professionals reports more outgoing than incoming movements for Nigeria, Morocco and South Africa in 2022. The published summary gives ratio bands, not exact counts, and the population is broader than AI specialists. [6]

    Normalised illustration of the reported ratio band. The slider does not reveal observed numbers of people, simulate policy effects or estimate today’s flows. Data period: 2022; explanatory article published 2 March 2026. LinkedIn coverage is selective.

    Training is one part of the institution.

    CMU-Africa’s institutional overview reports that 85% of its graduates work on the continent. It offers engineering, IT and AI degrees; the figure is not confined to an AI graduating class, and the overview does not specify its measurement date or cohort. Still, it gives us a concrete institutional question to investigate: what arrangements allow advanced training to connect with opportunities nearby? [7]

    Scholarships can widen who enters a programme. Supervisors, research funding, equipment, credible employers and a route into paid work help determine what can happen after it. We should be interested in the whole sequence. Counting enrolments at the beginning tells us little about employment at the end unless the stages are followed.

    Caribou’s 2026 study draws on 332 youth interviews in seven countries. It describes the importance of affordable access, dependable infrastructure and peer-learning routes alongside skills. Its purposive sample is qualitative, so it supports analysis of barriers rather than national estimates of how many people face each one. [8]

    A second unit of analysis

    People need places to do the work.

    A university, a scholarship programme and a company office are different kinds of infrastructure for talent. Explore the documented examples separately from the person sample.

    Institution entries are examples, not a directory or headcount. A listed training programme does not establish how many graduates remain in a country.

    The opportunity

    What would make it possible to keep building?

    The useful test of a talent strategy is what becomes possible after the training announcement: a funded research post, a product team with customers, a student who can access a supervisor, or an experiment that can be repeated without exhausting its budget.

    For institutions trying to build those conditions, the evidence to collect is quite practical. Follow funded places through completion and employment. Record whether research grants support teams beyond an initial project. Examine where firms commission technical work and who owns the resulting products. Document collaboration as well as movement.

    The paths in this essay show several ways people connect African institutions to one another and to organisations elsewhere. The broader sources show community reach, a specific institution’s reported graduate outcomes and selective labour-market flows. Together they make a case for examining the arrangements around talent. They do not resolve how much talent the continent has, or establish why any particular person chooses to move.

    That is the next reporting task: a wider, country-by-country account of people, employers and research institutions, with enough continuity to distinguish a temporary affiliation from a durable change.

    Method, inclusion rules and sources

    Roles are non-exclusive editorial categories based on documented work. Country connections can mean explicitly stated origin, education or organisational founding. They are never inferred from names. Institutional work affiliation does not establish residence, citizenship or remote-work status. Source-check dates are retrieval dates, not the dates a person started a role.

    Education stages may be incomplete and include non-AI degrees preceding AI work; these are not AI-graduate counts. A missing university can mean unreviewed information, not a missing qualification. Self-directed learning is recorded as such. No private contact details are included.
